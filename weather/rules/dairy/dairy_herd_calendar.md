# Kenya Dairy Herd Management Calendar

**For agronomist review**
Version 1.0 | 30 April 2026

This calendar describes the month-by-month management activities, disease and parasite risks, and herd lifecycle stages for smallholder dairy farming in Kenya. It is used by the advisory system to determine what management phase the herd is in and what tasks are due, so that weather-triggered advice is relevant to the time of year.

**Sources referenced throughout:**
- **Dairy Farmers Handbook**, 9th ed. 2025, Farmers Helping Farmers/UPEI
- **Feeding Manual**, ILRI/SDP/KDDP, 2007
- **Smallholder Dairy Farmer Training Manual**, ILRI Manual 24, 2016

**Target animal:** Friesian × Zebu crossbred (dominant smallholder type)

**Important notes:**
- Calendar calibrated to Friesian × Zebu crossbred dairy cattle, the most common commercial smallholder type in Kenya
- Timing shifts by ±2–3 weeks depending on altitude, microclimate, and farm management
- Calendar follows Kenya's bimodal rainfall pattern (long rains Mar–May, short rains Oct–Dec)

---

## Kenya Seasons

| Season | Months | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Long Rains | March – May | Main wet season. High disease pressure. Calving season. Peak lactation. |
| Long Dry | June – September | Cool/dry. Breeding season. Heat stress risk peaks Jul–Aug. |
| Short Rains | October – December | Secondary wet season. Tick pressure surges. Drying off begins. |
| Short Dry | January – February | Driest period. Pre-calving management critical. |

## Monthly Calendar

### January — Short Dry Season

**Herd stage:** Dry period

| Priority | Task | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Critical** | Housing maintenance | Repair and clean housing during dry season — fix leaking roofs, broken drainage, and rotting floors before the long rains arrive. Ensure bedding is dry to prevent mastitis. |
| **High** | Vaccination | Administer FMD booster vaccination (first of two annual doses). Record animals vaccinated and due dates for the September dose. |
| **High** | Deworming | Strategic deworming at start of short dry season. Use broad-spectrum anthelmintic (e.g. albendazole or ivermectin). Rotate drug class annually to reduce resistance. |
| **High** | Supplementary feeding | Dry season forage quality and quantity declines sharply. Supplement with stored hay, silage, or concentrate to maintain body condition score (BCS) 3.0–3.5 ahead of calving. |
| **Medium** | Acaricide spraying | Fortnightly tick control programme during the dry season. Rotate acaricide class (e.g. amidine, synthetic pyrethroid) to manage resistance. |
| **Medium** | Body condition scoring | Score all cows in dry period. Target BCS 3.0–3.5 at calving. Adjust ration now — it is too late to correct condition score in the final 3 weeks before calving. |

**Disease risk:**

| Disease | Risk Level |
|---|---|
| Tick-borne disease | Low |
| Mastitis | Low |
| Heat stress | Low |
| Respiratory pneumonia | Medium |

**Parasite risk:**

| Parasite | Risk Level |
|---|---|
| Ticks | Low |
| Internal worms | Medium |

**Nutrition notes:** Forage quality lowest of the year. Provide hay or silage at minimum 3% of body weight (dry matter). Supplement with 1–2 kg/day dairy meal for cows approaching calving. Ensure continuous access to clean water (min 50 L/cow/day).

### February — Short Dry Season

**Herd stage:** Pre-calving

| Priority | Task | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Critical** | Pre-calving management | Implement 'steaming up' — increase concentrate to 2–3 kg/day in the 3 weeks before expected calving. Ensure cows calve at BCS 3.0–3.5. Separate cows due to calve within 1 week into a dedicated, clean, well-bedded calving pen. |
| **High** | Housing prep | Deep-clean and disinfect calving pens before the calving season. Lay fresh dry bedding (straw or wood shavings). Ensure calving area is sheltered from cold nights. |
| **High** | Vaccination | Administer Blackquarter (BQ) and Haemorrhagic Septicaemia (HS) annual vaccination. Conduct brucellosis check/certification — important for milk safety and herd certification programmes. |
| **Medium** | Acaricide spraying | Continue fortnightly tick control. Inspect udder and groin for tick burdens in pre-calving cows — high tick loads can cause stress during late pregnancy. |
| **Medium** | Water access | Ensure all dry cows and heifers have clean water at all times. Dehydration in late pregnancy elevates retained placenta and calving difficulty risk. |

**Disease risk:**

| Disease | Risk Level |
|---|---|
| Tick-borne disease | Low |
| Mastitis | Low |
| Heat stress | Low |
| Respiratory pneumonia | Medium |

**Parasite risk:**

| Parasite | Risk Level |
|---|---|
| Ticks | Low |
| Internal worms | Medium |

**Nutrition notes:** Steaming-up period: increase energy-dense concentrate to 2–3 kg/day for 3 weeks pre-calving. Do NOT reduce roughage — fibre intake maintains rumen health and reduces milk fever risk. Avoid over-conditioning (BCS >3.5) which increases ketosis and calving problems.

### March — Long Rains

**Herd stage:** Calving season

| Priority | Task | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Critical** | Calving management | Attend all calvings. Feed newborn calf colostrum within 6 hours of birth (minimum 2 litres, ideally 3–4 litres). Record calving date, calf sex, and dam ID. Check for retained placenta (remove manually after 12 hours if not expelled). Monitor cows for milk fever and prolapse. |
| **Critical** | Acaricide spraying | Switch to weekly acaricide spraying as long rains begin — tick activity surges with rising humidity and vegetation. Pay special attention to calves, which have lower tick immunity. |
| **High** | Mastitis monitoring | Conduct California Mastitis Test (CMT) on all freshly-calved cows within 1 week of calving. Observe foremilk before each milking for clots, watery milk, or blood. Treat clinical mastitis promptly with intramammary antibiotics. |
| **High** | Calf management | House calves separately from cows after colostrum feeding. Feed whole milk or milk replacer at 10% of body weight/day in two feeds. Offer calf starter concentrate and clean water from day 3. |
| **Medium** | Pasture management | Long rains signal rapid pasture regrowth. Avoid overgrazing — allow 21–28 days rest between grazings. Do not graze paddocks less than 15 cm tall to prevent soil poaching and re-infection with parasites. |

**Disease risk:**

| Disease | Risk Level |
|---|---|
| Tick-borne disease | High |
| Mastitis | High |
| Heat stress | Low |
| Respiratory pneumonia | Low |

**Parasite risk:**

| Parasite | Risk Level |
|---|---|
| Ticks | High |
| Internal worms | Rising |

**Nutrition notes:** Freshly-calved cows enter peak nutritional demand. Transition to full lactation ration: 2–3 kg dairy meal per 5 litres of milk produced above 5 litres maintenance. Rising pasture quality during long rains reduces supplement requirement — assess weekly.

### April — Long Rains (Peak)

**Herd stage:** Calving season

| Priority | Task | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Critical** | Acaricide spraying | Maintain weekly acaricide programme. April is peak tick season — failure to spray increases East Coast Fever (ECF), Anaplasmosis, and Babesiosis risk dramatically. Check calves daily for tick burdens, especially around the ears and groin. |
| **Critical** | Mastitis monitoring | Wet conditions and calving combine to create peak mastitis risk. Use CMT weekly on all lactating cows. Ensure post-milking teat dipping in iodine-glycerine solution (0.3–0.5% iodine). Keep bedding clean and dry — add fresh material daily in wet weather. |
| **High** | Forage management | Implement rotational grazing strictly to prevent overgrazing and pasture damage from wet-season poaching. Begin Napier grass and legume (e.g. Desmodium) planting in well-drained plots for dry season forage conservation. |
| **High** | Calf health | Wet, cold conditions increase calf scours (diarrhoea) and pneumonia risk. Ensure calf housing is draught-free, dry, and well-ventilated. Treat scours with oral rehydration salts — seek veterinary advice for bloody scours. |
| **High** | Milking hygiene | Wash udder with warm water and dry before milking. Use pre- and post-milking teat dip. Strip foremilk into a separate strip cup — do not contaminate the bulk milk with mastitic milk. |

**Disease risk:**

| Disease | Risk Level |
|---|---|
| Tick-borne disease | Very High |
| Mastitis | Very High |
| Heat stress | Low |
| Respiratory pneumonia | Medium |

**Parasite risk:**

| Parasite | Risk Level |
|---|---|
| Ticks | Very High |
| Internal worms | Rising |

**Nutrition notes:** Peak lactation beginning for cows that calved in March. Maintain high-energy ration — under-nutrition now depresses milk yield for the entire lactation. Green pasture quality is high during long rains; balance with concentrate to avoid rumen acidosis from excess lush grass.

### May — Long Rains (Ending)

**Herd stage:** Peak lactation

| Priority | Task | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Critical** | Acaricide spraying | Continue weekly acaricide spraying through end of long rains. As rains taper, tick populations peak before the dry-season decline — do not reduce frequency prematurely. |
| **High** | Deworming | Strategic post-long-rains deworming. Pasture larval burden peaks after wet season — treat all lactating cows, dry cows, and calves. Use anthelmintic class not used in January dose. |
| **High** | Silage making | OPTIMAL WINDOW for Napier grass silage — abundant, high-quality green material available. Harvest at 1–1.2 m height, chop to 2–3 cm, compact well in pits or bags, seal airtight. Target 4–6 tonnes DM per cow for dry season. |
| **Medium** | Heat stress monitoring | As rains end, watch for rising temperatures combined with humidity. Signs of heat stress: increased respiration rate (>60 breaths/min), reduced feed intake, clustering near water. Ensure shade and fresh water access. |
| **Medium** | Breeding records | Record cows that calved in February–March and are now approaching optimal AI window (50–80 days post-calving). Observe for oestrus signs (restlessness, clear discharge, standing to be mounted). |

**Disease risk:**

| Disease | Risk Level |
|---|---|
| Tick-borne disease | High |
| Mastitis | Medium |
| Heat stress | Medium |
| Respiratory pneumonia | Low |

**Parasite risk:**

| Parasite | Risk Level |
|---|---|
| Ticks | Very High |
| Internal worms | High |

**Nutrition notes:** Cows in peak lactation (2–3 months post-calving) have the highest energy demand of the productive cycle. Do not reduce concentrate below 1 kg per 3–4 litres of milk. Cut and carry fresh Napier grass to supplement declining pasture quality as dry season approaches.

### June — Long Dry Season

**Herd stage:** Peak lactation

| Priority | Task | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Critical** | Breeding AI | Priority AI breeding month for cows that calved in February–March and are now 50–80 days post-calving (optimal conception window). Detect oestrus twice daily (morning and evening). Call AI technician within 6–12 hours of confirmed standing oestrus. Target 365-day calving interval. |
| **High** | Acaricide spraying | Reduce to fortnightly as dry season reduces tick activity. Do not stop entirely — monitor tick burdens weekly and spray immediately if ticks found in numbers. |
| **High** | Forage conservation | Harvest mature Napier grass and Rhodes grass for hay making. Dry hay in windrows for 3–5 days before baling or storing loose. Target minimum 200–300 kg hay per cow for dry season feeding. |
| **Medium** | Water management | Dry season water stress begins. Ensure water points are clean and cattle have access at least twice daily. Minimum 50 L/day for dry cows, 80–100 L/day for lactating cows. |
| **Medium** | Milk quality | Establish good milking records — record daily milk weight per cow. Identify cows declining faster than expected (possible subclinical mastitis or nutrition gap). Milk at consistent times — irregular milking intervals reduce yield. |

**Disease risk:**

| Disease | Risk Level |
|---|---|
| Tick-borne disease | High |
| Mastitis | Medium |
| Heat stress | High |
| Respiratory pneumonia | Low |

**Parasite risk:**

| Parasite | Risk Level |
|---|---|
| Ticks | Medium |
| Internal worms | Medium |

**Nutrition notes:** Transition to dry season feeding. Green pasture declining rapidly. Supplement lactating cows with 2–3 kg/day dairy meal plus cut-and-carry Napier. Begin rationing stored silage/hay — calculate stock against expected dry season duration (June–September).

### July — Long Dry Season

**Herd stage:** Breeding season

| Priority | Task | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Critical** | Breeding AI | Continue AI programme for cows not yet confirmed pregnant. For late calvers (March–April), this is the optimal AI window. Maintain accurate oestrus detection records. If cow fails to conceive after 3 AI attempts, arrange veterinary examination. |
| **Critical** | Forage conservation | Maximise hay and silage production while any green material remains. Prioritise Napier, fodder maize, and legume hays. Store in dry, raised, rodent-proof locations. Label date of storage — use oldest stock first. |
| **Critical** | Water management | July is peak dry season — water scarcity is most acute. Ensure water troughs are filled daily. Where boreholes or piped water are unavailable, arrange water trucking before crisis point. Dehydration reduces milk yield by 15–25% and conception rates by 10–20%. |
| **High** | Heat stress management | Peak heat stress months (Jul–Aug). Provide shade trees or shade structures over resting areas. Graze in early morning and late afternoon; allow rest during peak heat (10 am–3 pm). Monitor respiration rates — above 80 breaths/min is severe stress. |
| **Medium** | Acaricide spraying | Fortnightly tick control. Tick pressure is medium during dry season but do not abandon the programme — disease-carrying ticks are present year-round. |

**Disease risk:**

| Disease | Risk Level |
|---|---|
| Tick-borne disease | High |
| Mastitis | Low |
| Heat stress | Very High |
| Respiratory pneumonia | Low |

**Parasite risk:**

| Parasite | Risk Level |
|---|---|
| Ticks | Medium |
| Internal worms | Medium |

**Nutrition notes:** Dry season feeding at full effect. Ration stored forage carefully — do not feed more than needed. Supplement with 2 kg/day dairy meal per lactating cow. Urea-treated straw (3% urea solution, 7-day fermentation) can extend low-quality roughage value. Monitor BCS monthly.

### August — Long Dry Season

**Herd stage:** Breeding season

| Priority | Task | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Pregnancy confirmation | Pregnancy diagnose cows bred in June–July (60–90 days post-AI is optimal for rectal palpation or ultrasound by vet/AI technician). Cows confirmed in calf: plan dry-off date (lactation days 275–285). Cows not in calf: arrange repeat AI or consider culling decision. |
| **High** | Forage planting prep | Prepare land for forage planting in anticipation of short rains (October). Apply manure and phosphate fertiliser to plots. Plan Napier splits, Sudan grass, and fodder legume seed stocks. |
| **Critical** | Water management | Continue rigorous water provision. August is the second peak dry month. Check water source levels — rainwater harvesting tanks should be monitored. Prioritise lactating cows and late-pregnant animals for water access. |
| **High** | Heat stress management | Continued heat stress peak. Avoid heavy physical work (herding, loading) during midday heat. If night temperatures stay above 20°C, supplement energy in the ration as cattle cannot eat during peak heat. |
| **Medium** | Acaricide spraying | Maintain fortnightly tick spraying programme. As short rains approach in October, tick populations will surge again — a robust programme now prevents re-infestation. |

Cows in late lactation (month 8–10) have lower milk yield and can tolerate slightly reduced concentrate, but forage should remain ad libitum.

### September — Long Dry Season (End)

**Herd stage:** Late lactation

| Priority | Task | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Forage planting | Plant fast-establishing fodder crops ahead of short rains — Boma Rhodes grass, Brachiaria (Mulato II), Sudan grass, and fodder cowpea. Sow in prepared seedbeds. Napier splits can be planted now for establishment before rains. |
| **High** | Vaccination | Annual FMD vaccination — second dose of the year. Record all animals vaccinated. Timing before the short rains (October) is strategic as animal movement increases disease spread risk. |
| **High** | Housing maintenance | Pre-rains housing inspection and maintenance. Repair all roof leaks, drainage channels, and floor drainage before October rains arrive. Replace bedding material — accumulated dry-season bedding must be composted. |
| **Medium** | Acaricide spraying | Fortnightly acaricide application continues. September is the last month of lower tick pressure — prepare stocks of acaricide for the weekly programme starting in October. |
| **Medium** | Dry-off planning | Identify cows due for drying off in October (10 months post-calving or as planned). Begin reducing milkings from 2x/day to 1x/day in the final 2 weeks before dry-off. Do not abruptly stop milking high-yielding cows. |

### October — Short Rains

**Herd stage:** Drying off

| Priority | Task | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Critical** | Drying off | Implement dry-off for cows completing 305-day lactation. At final milking, infuse ALL four quarters with Dry Cow Therapy (DCT) antibiotic tubes (e.g. cloxacillin or amoxicillin long-acting). Apply teat sealant after DCT infusion. Move dried-off cows to separate, clean dry-cow group. |
| **Critical** | Acaricide spraying | Switch back to weekly acaricide spraying as short rains begin — tick populations surge. October–November is second peak tick season. Ensure all animals are sprayed, including recently dried-off cows and young stock. |
| **High** | Forage planting | SHORT RAINS PLANTING WINDOW — plant Napier grass splits, Brachiaria, fodder legumes (Desmodium, Leucaena), and fodder maize for silage. Establish now to have forage ready for the next dry season. |
| **High** | Mastitis surveillance | Inspect dry cows for swollen, painful quarters in first 2 weeks after dry-off — indicates DCT failure or new infection. Monitor closely as wet season begins. Maintain post-milking teat dip on lactating cows. |
| **Medium** | Calf health | Calves born in June–August are now 2–4 months old. Check growth rates and health status. Begin weaning calves from milk at 3–4 months by gradual reduction. Offer good quality starter concentrate and pasture. |

### November — Short Rains

**Herd stage:** Drying off

| Priority | Task | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Critical** | Dry cow management | Monitor all dry cows daily for signs of mastitis, vaginal discharge, or poor body condition. Check that teat canal seal (from DCT/teat sealant) is intact — quarantine any cow showing udder swelling for veterinary treatment. Provide adequate nutrition for BCS gain if needed. |
| **Critical** | Acaricide spraying | Maintain weekly acaricide spraying through peak short-rains tick season. Monitor for acaricide resistance — if ticks survive 48 hours post-spray, switch to a different chemical class. East Coast Fever is the most economically damaging tick-borne disease in Kenya. |
| **High** | Deworming | Post-short-rains strategic deworming (third annual dose). Treat all categories: lactating cows, dry cows, and calves. High pasture larval burden after October–November rains. Use a third anthelmintic class if possible. |
| **Medium** | Forage assessment | Assess forage planted in October — Napier splits establishing, fodder legumes germinating. Topdress with CAN (50 kg/acre) to accelerate growth. Plan first harvest of new Napier at 4 cuts/year cycle. |
| **Medium** | Pre-calving prep | Cows dried off in October are now 4–5 weeks into dry period. Confirm calving dates for December–January calvers. Clean and prepare calving pens. Ensure colostrum plan is in place (bank colostrum from high producers if needed). |

### December — Short Rains (Ending)

**Herd stage:** Dry period

| Priority | Task | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Housing maintenance | End-of-year housing inspection and deep clean. Compost manure heaps. Fix all drainage and floor issues identified during the wet season. Prepare the farm record system — review production records, breeding records, and health treatments for the year. |
| **High** | Vaccination | Annual vaccination review — confirm all animals received FMD (twice), BQ/HS (once), and any leptospirosis or lumpy skin disease vaccines recommended locally. Plan vaccination calendar for the coming year. |
| **High** | Body condition scoring | Score all cows — dry cows should be BCS 3.0–3.5 heading into January calving season. Cows below BCS 2.5 need intensive supplementation immediately. Cows above BCS 3.5 should be restricted to avoid metabolic problems at calving. |
| **Medium** | Acaricide spraying | Short rains tapering in December. Weekly spraying continues while vegetation remains lush. Switch to fortnightly in late December as conditions dry. Stock adequate acaricide for the January–February programme. |
| **Medium** | Herd records | Complete annual herd records. Calculate: calving rate, conception rate, milk yield per cow per year, calf mortality, disease incidence. Use data to identify underperforming animals and plan culling and replacement decisions. |

## How This Calendar Is Used by the Advisory System

This calendar is **not sent to the LLM**. It runs in the Python rule engine:

1. The system looks up the current month → determines herd stage for the farmer's herd
2. Management tasks that don't apply to the current herd stage are filtered out (e.g., no calving management advice in June — herd is in peak lactation, not calving season)
3. Tasks marked "critical" or "high" for this month are converted into weather-gated advice:
   - "Drying off is due" + dry weather = "Good time to plan dry-off"
   - "Acaricide spraying is due" + rain = "Acaricide spraying is due this month — ideal timing as ticks are active in wet conditions"
   - "Vaccination is due" + suitable weather = "Plan vaccination clinic this week"
4. Disease/pest risk levels generate seasonal alerts when elevated (e.g., "Tick-borne disease risk is HIGH — ensure spraying is on schedule")
5. Only the resulting triggered advice (typically 5–10 items) goes into the LLM prompt for translation into farmer-friendly WhatsApp language
